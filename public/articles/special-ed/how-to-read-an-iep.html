How to read a Massachusetts IEP

What an IEP actually is

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) is a legally binding written commitment — not a wish list. Under the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) and Massachusetts regulation 603 CMR 28.00, if it isn't written into the IEP, the district generally isn't required to provide it. That fact changes how you should read every page: the IEP is the contract, and the meeting notes are not.

Massachusetts uses a standardized IEP form. Sections may be ordered differently by form edition, but the substance is the same.

The 8 required parts of an IEP

Every IEP must contain these elements (603 CMR 28.05):

  1. Present levels of performance (PLAAFP). Where your child is right now academically and functionally — backed by evaluations, classroom data, and observations. Everything else in the IEP should build from here.
  2. Measurable annual goals. What your child can reasonably accomplish in a year, with a baseline and a way to measure it (e.g., "from 40 to 70 words read per minute").
  3. Progress reporting. How progress toward each goal will be measured and how often you'll be told — in Massachusetts, at least as often as report cards.
  4. Special education services. The specialized instruction itself, each with a frequency, duration, and location (e.g., "reading intervention, 4× per week, 45 minutes, resource room").
  5. Related services. Speech and language, occupational therapy, counseling, transportation — any service your child needs to benefit from their education.
  6. Accommodations and modifications. Accommodations change how your child learns (extra time, audiobooks); modifications change what they're expected to learn. Both are only meaningful if specific enough to actually use.
  7. Participation with nondisabled peers. An explanation of the extent — if any — your child won't participate in regular education classes and activities (the "least restrictive environment" analysis).
  8. Placement decision. The setting where services happen — inclusion, substantially separate, or somewhere between — based on your child's needs, not district convenience.

Where applicable, the IEP must also address extended school year (ESY) services, MCAS participation, and — beginning no later than age 14 in Massachusetts — transition planning. If your child qualifies and the page is blank, that's a completeness problem.

Practical tip: Read the IEP with the evaluation reports beside it. Every goal should trace back to a need named in the present levels. If a goal isn't connected to documented data, ask the team to explain the evidence behind it — if they can't, that goal is unlikely to survive a dispute.

How to check if your child's IEP is complete

  • Every required section is filled in — no blank boxes, no "see meeting notes."
  • Every goal has a baseline, a target, and a measurement method.
  • Every service has frequency, duration, and location.
  • Accommodations are specific (not just "extended time" — how much?).
  • The present levels match the evaluations and what you see at home.
  • It's dated this school year and reflects your child's current grade and team.

Red flags to look for

  • Copy-paste language — check for last year's dates or data that doesn't match your child.
  • Vague goals with no baseline or measurement ("will improve reading skills").
  • Services without frequency, duration, or location — these are unenforceable.
  • "As needed" accommodations — nobody can tell whether they were provided.
  • Blank ESY or transition pages when your child qualifies.
  • Pressure to sign on the spot — signing is not a waiver; you can always take the IEP home to read before agreeing.

What to do next

  1. Get the documents. You're entitled to a copy of the IEP and evaluation reports at no cost — ask in writing if you don't have them.
  2. Write down your concerns before the next Team meeting — bring a one-page list, not a speech.
  3. Request changes in writing. Ask for a Team meeting to discuss anything you disagree with — the district must convene one.
  4. Escalate if needed. If the district won't fix a problem, Massachusetts parents can file a complaint with DESE's Problem Resolution System — our guide covers when that makes sense.
  5. Keep records safe. IEPs contain sensitive information — if you share documents, make sure redactions actually work first.
