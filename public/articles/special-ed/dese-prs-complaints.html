DESE PRS complaints — what they are and when to file

Aug 2026 · 7 min read

If your child's school district broke the special education rules, you don't need a lawyer to get the state to look at it. A complaint to DESE's PRS office is the free, parent-friendly way to put a violation on the official record — and get a written state finding on whether the district broke the law.

What the PRS is

The PRS — Program Quality Assurance Services — is the office inside the Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) that investigates complaints against school districts and other public agencies. When someone files a special education complaint, the PRS is the unit that investigates it.

The process comes from 603 CMR 28.08, the procedural-safeguards section of Massachusetts special education regulations, which tracks the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The PRS is not a court and not a hearing officer. It is an investigative office: it gathers documents, talks to the people involved, and issues a written decision about whether the district violated the law.

PRS complaint vs due process hearing vs mediation

There are three different roads for resolving special education disputes, and families often confuse them:

  • PRS complaint — You allege the district broke the law: a missed evaluation timeline, an IEP not implemented, a refused service, records withheld. It is free, informal, and you don't need an attorney. The state investigates and issues written findings.
  • Due process hearing — A formal, adversarial hearing before the Bureau of Special Education Appeals (BSEA) about your child's identification, evaluation, placement, or FAPE. There are rules of evidence, witnesses, and usually lawyers. The hearing officer's decision is binding.
  • Mediation — A voluntary, confidential session with a neutral mediator who helps both sides negotiate. Nothing becomes binding unless you both sign an agreement. You can request it at any time, and it costs nothing to try.

You are not limited to one. A PRS complaint can run alongside mediation or a hearing — and a PRS finding that the district violated the law can be powerful evidence in the others.

When to file (and when not to)

File when you can point to a specific, documented violation: a deadline the district missed, a service on the IEP that wasn't delivered, a refusal to evaluate, a failure to provide notice or records. File even when the problem seems small — small violations are still violations, and patterns matter.

Don't file when your real dispute is a disagreement about what services are "appropriate" — that is a FAPE question for the BSEA, not a PRS finding. Don't file on a violation more than a year old: complaints must generally be brought within one year of the alleged violation. And don't file to punish or vent — the district sees every complaint, and a sloppy or retaliatory one undercuts your credibility on the cases that matter.

What you need before filing

A complaint doesn't need to be a legal document, but it needs to be complete. Gather:

  • Your child's name, school, and district.
  • A plain-language description of the violation: what happened, when, and who was involved.
  • The documents that prove it: IEPs, evaluations, emails, meeting notices, service logs.
  • A short timeline of any attempts you made to resolve the issue.
  • Your contact information and signature.

You don't need a lawyer, and you don't need to be "right" about the law — describe the facts and let the state apply the rules.

The filing process step by step

  1. Get the PRS complaint form from DESE's website, or write a letter with the same information — there is no magic format.
  2. Fill in every required field: the alleged violation, the facts, and the name of the district.
  3. Sign it. An unsigned complaint may not be accepted.
  4. Submit it to the PRS by email or mail, and keep a dated copy of exactly what you sent.
  5. The PRS assigns a complaint number and an investigator. From that point, the state's clock is running.

What happens after filing

The PRS must investigate and issue a written decision — a Letter of Finding — within 60 calendar days of receiving the complaint, unless exceptional circumstances apply. The investigator reviews your documents, may ask the district for its records, and may interview you or district staff.

The Letter of Finding states whether the district violated the law. If it did, the letter orders corrective action: a fix for your child, a fix for the district's practices, or both, with a deadline. The PRS may follow up to verify the district actually complied. If your complaint also raises an issue that belongs at the BSEA — like a FAPE dispute — the PRS may defer that piece to the hearing process rather than decide it.

Tip: keep a dated copy of everything you submit to the PRS. If a letter goes missing or the state is slow, your records are what prove what you filed and when — and the 60-day investigation clock starts the day the PRS receives your complaint.

Practical tips

  • Keep the paper trail from day one — emails, notices, logs — before you ever need it.
  • Be specific about dates, documents, and people in your complaint.
  • Mind the deadlines: one year to file, 60 days for the state's finding.
  • File even if you're also in mediation or a hearing — the processes run in parallel.
  • Read the Letter of Finding carefully and save it. If the district is ordered to fix something, that letter is your enforcement record.
  • A finding isn't the end of the road. If you disagree with how the PRS resolved your complaint, ask about next steps.

Informational only — not legal advice. Rules and DESE procedures change; verify current details with DESE or an advocate before you file.
